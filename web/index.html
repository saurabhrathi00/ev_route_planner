Leave traffic on Work it out for me and the app decides from your departure time — with a Google key it uses the delay Google measured for that hour, otherwise a rule of thumb from the clock. Override it if you know better than both.

Leave departure empty for now. Setting it matters more than it looks: the app fetches the temperature and wind for the hour you will actually be at each point, and a hot afternoon can cost several points of charge more than the same drive at dawn.

Chargers are off by default — the plan still tells you where you drop to your floor, what to charge to and where to look. Switch it on to have the app find and rank actual chargers, which adds a few seconds.

Speed comes from your driving style — gentle means both softer acceleration and a lower cruise. The router's own speed for each road still wins where it is lower, so nobody cruises at 82 km/h through a village.

Where data comes from

google or free

Leave this On. It is the default and what the app is tuned for: better place search for Indian town names, chargers that the open databases miss entirely outside the metros, star ratings and live free-bay counts where the network reports them, and a measured traffic delay instead of a guess.

Turning it Off uses only open data — OpenStreetMap, Open Charge Map, OpenRouteService. Everything still works and the physics is identical; you lose ratings, live bay counts, and chargers in thinly mapped areas. There is nothing to pay either way — this is not a paid tier, and no key of yours is involved.

Either way the app falls back on its own whenever a service is slow or unavailable, so a plan never fails for want of one.

Nothing about your drive leaves this device.
  • There is no server. This app has no backend of any kind and no account behind it. Every calculation happens on this device.
  • Your trip log, calibration and cached terrain are written to this device's local storage and nowhere else. Uninstalling erases them.
  • Requests go to the mapping and charger services listed above and nowhere else. Your route is never sent to us, because there is no us to send it to.

Do not take our word for it — the whole app is one readable file: read the source.

Reverse place-naming always uses the free service: Google's Geocoding API refuses browser requests, so it cannot be called from here at all.

The physics needs three things from a car: how much charge it really holds, what it weighs, and how hard it is to push through air. The first two are published. The third almost never is, so it starts as an estimate from the body shape — and an estimate from a shape is exactly the sort of thing that turns out wrong.

So where a car has been through a measured range test, that test is worked back through this model to find what it actually costs to move, and the difference is carried as a correction. Bigger than 1.00 means the car uses more than the shape suggests — heavy SUVs mostly do, and the largest here was out by a third.

Measured figures are from Autocar India's real-world range tests — their measurements, our arithmetic. Cars they have not tested carry 1.09, the middle of the ones they have, because most cars turn out to use more than the shape alone predicts and 1.00 would be a guess in the wrong direction.

None of this is as good as your own drive. Log one and the correction stands down — your car's own numbers replace the estimate for it entirely.

Two real trips in the same car fix the model. Trip A should climb; trip B should be flat. Driving time matters more than anything else here — the model needs the average speed each trip actually ran at, not the speed you held on the good bits.
